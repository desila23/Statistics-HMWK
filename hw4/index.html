01 / INTRODUCTION

One attacker is a path.
Many paths form a distribution.

Consider n computer systems and m attackers. Each attacker makes exactly one attempt against each system, giving n random increments along one path. All m attackers follow the same increment law, independently in the mathematical model.

A stochastic process is a family of random quantities indexed by time or step. Here Sₜ = X₁ + ⋯ + Xₜ is the score after t systems. The increments are i.i.d.: independent, so previous attempts do not change the next attempt’s probability, and identically distributed, so the same law applies at every step and to every attacker.

Bernoulli increments count successful attempts: a failure adds zero. Rademacher increments model a signed score: each attempt adds +1 or −1 with equal probability. A negative Rademacher score is a net signed score, not a negative number of compromised systems. We study the total, the running average and the square-root-normalized score because each highlights a different aspect of randomness.

i

A mathematical model, not an intrusion-detection system. Real attacks share vulnerabilities and respond to changing defences. These dependencies and changing success rates are deliberately excluded here.

Sₙ follows a Binomial(n, p) law. It never decreases along a path. At p = 0 or 1 the process is deterministic and all three variances are zero.

Balance gains and losses.

If K is Binomial(n, ½), then Sₙ = 2K − n. Scores have the same parity as n and lie on a lattice with spacing 2. Individual paths can move in either direction.

Why does dividing by n differ from dividing by √n?

Independence makes variances add: Var(Sₙ) = Σ Var(Xᵢ) = nσ². Scaling a random variable by a multiplies its variance by a². Consequently, dividing Sₙ by n divides its variance by n², leaving σ²/n. Dividing by √n divides it by n, leaving σ². These identities hold for every finite n, before any limiting theorem is used.

The theorem, the bound and the sampling design
P(|Sₙ/n − μ| > ε) → 0
P(|Sₙ/n − μ| > ε) ≤ min(1, σ²/(nε²))

For every fixed ε > 0, the theoretical Chebyshev bound tends to zero. This establishes convergence in probability under the stated assumptions. The graphs illustrate this result; they do not prove it. An average need not move closer to μ on every step.

Each attacker is simulated once up to the maximum n. Checkpoints reuse prefixes of the same paths, so comparisons across n are paired. At a fixed n the m attackers are the independent observational units of the model. Wilson intervals describe Monte Carlo uncertainty of the estimated deviation frequency; they do not describe uncertainty about the known μ. A zero observed count does not prove a zero probability. The finite-n exact probability comes from the binomial law, not from the normal approximation.

05 / VARIANCE ANALYSIS

Three rescalings.
Three variance laws.

What are we measuring? Dispersion across attackers, using the increasing-n experiment above. Solid curves show exact theory; points show sample variances computed from the simulated scores.

GROWS LINEARLY

Var(Sₙ) = nσ²

Longer totals fluctuate more in absolute score units. Standard deviation grows as σ√n.

SHRINKS AS 1/n

Var(Sₙ/n) = σ²/n

Averaging divides by n and therefore divides variance by n². Relative noise contracts.

STAYS CONSTANT

Var(Sₙ/√n) = σ²

Square-root normalization balances the variance growth. It does not centre a nonzero mean.

A discrete walk.

CDF gaps are descriptive suprema, including both sides of each discrete jump. No Kolmogorov–Smirnov p-value is claimed. The exact gap separates finite-n discreteness from Monte Carlo noise.

RADEMACHER CLT

Convergence in distribution

Snn→dN(0,1)

At every finite n the score remains discrete. Convergence in distribution means that P(Zₙ ≤ x) approaches Φ(x) at each continuity point of the limiting CDF. It does not mean that Zₙ converges in probability to 0. Its variance stays 1.

φ(x) = exp(−x²/2) / √(2π)

The displayed Gaussian is the standard normal density, with no fitted parameters. Histogram bars are densities too, so their total area is one.

BERNOULLI DISTINCTION

Normalizing is not centring.

For Bernoulli with p > 0, E[Sₙ/√n] = p√n grows. That uncentred quantity does not converge to N(0, 1). For fixed 0 < p < 1, the correctly centred and standardized variable is:

Sn−npnp(1−p)→dN(0,1)

This optional Bernoulli extension is explained here, not plotted as if it were the Rademacher experiment. At p = 0 or 1 the standardization is undefined because the variance is zero.

07 / THE EFFECT OF n AND m

Change the process.
Or change how much you observe.

Increasing n changes the score distribution of a single attacker. Increasing m at fixed n leaves that law unchanged and gives us more observations with which to estimate it.

The groups are nested: m = 50 uses the first 50 of the same 5,000 paths. All histograms use the same bin boundaries. Total variation is computed on the full exact discrete support, not the display bins. A single realization need not improve monotonically with m.

Increasing n: a longer experiment

The expected total is nμ. Its variance is nσ², but averaging reduces variance to σ²/n. Square-root normalization preserves σ²; only the symmetric Rademacher case has mean zero without centring. More systems do not automatically mean better real-world security: n is a mathematical horizon here.

Increasing m: a larger ensemble

The distribution of one attacker’s Sₙ does not change. Its empirical histogram becomes less noisy in a large independent sample. The Monte Carlo standard error of the ensemble mean of Sₙ/√n is σ/√m. This is uncertainty of an estimator, not the standard deviation σ of one attacker’s normalized score.

We never add all attackers’ scores and label that sum as Sₙ. A combined score would be a different random variable, with nm increments under the model.

08 / STATISTICAL CONCLUSIONS

Separate the theorem
from the measured sample.

Different increments produce different paths. Bernoulli counts successes and has drift p with variance p(1 − p). Symmetric Rademacher has no drift and unit increment variance. The two models share the same rules for adding and scaling independent increments.

The LLN concerns concentration. Sₙ/n approaches μ in probability as n grows. The shrinking variance σ²/n and Chebyshev bound explain why deviations become unlikely. The ensemble mean can already be close to μ at a small n if m is large; that fact alone does not establish the LLN. Inspect the spread across attackers and the deviation probability.

Measured LLN results are being calculated.

Three scales retain different amounts of variation. The total-score variance grows as nσ², so absolute fluctuations have scale √n. The average-score variance falls as σ²/n, while the normalized-score variance stays σ². For Rademacher these are n, 1/n and 1; for Bernoulli multiply each by p(1 − p). A constant normalized variance does not imply a constant Bernoulli mean: that mean is p√n.

The CLT concerns distributional shape. For Rademacher, Sₙ/√n approaches N(0, 1), while remaining discrete for every finite n. Its variance is already 1; convergence is seen in the shape and CDF gap. A Bernoulli process needs centring and division by √(np(1 − p)) for a standard normal limit.

Measured CLT results are being calculated.

n and m answer different questions. n changes the per-attacker law. m changes the precision of the empirical distribution and moment estimates. Neither a smooth histogram nor one mean near its target proves a theorem. More attackers cannot repair biased sampling or a wrong increment model.

Limits remain explicit. These finite, seeded pseudorandom experiments idealize independence, identical probabilities, and static defences. Real attackers can adapt, share information and target correlated systems. Sample means, variances and tail frequencies fluctuate; the theory is exact under the assumptions, whereas numerical estimates have Monte Carlo error. No operational cyber-risk claim is made.

Short glossary
Expected value
The probability-weighted mean of a random variable, denoted E[X].
Variance
E[(X − E[X])²], the average squared distance from the mean.
Law of large numbers (LLN)
Averages of i.i.d. integrable increments concentrate around their common expected value as the number of steps grows.
Central limit theorem (CLT)
For i.i.d. increments with positive finite variance, the centred, standardized sum converges in distribution to N(0, 1).
Convergence in probability
For every fixed ε > 0, the probability of being more than ε away from a target tends to zero.
Convergence in distribution
The CDFs approach the target CDF at its continuity points; individual values need not approach a constant.
Reproducibility, numerical methods and primary sources

The simulation uses Mulberry32 with an unsigned 32-bit seed. Attackers are generated in attacker-major order, with exactly one pseudorandom uniform per increment. This approximates the mathematical i.i.d. model; a deterministic PRNG is not a proof of independence or a cryptographic generator. Same settings, seed and algorithm reproduce the same paths. Finite-n binomial masses are calculated using a recurrence around the mode and normalization, avoiding large factorials. Sample variance uses Welford’s algorithm.

Histograms group adjacent lattice cells and divide counts by m × bin width. The standard-normal line is a density. Normal CDF values use Abramowitz–Stegun approximation 26.2.17 (absolute error around 7.5 × 10⁻⁸ or less). No formal goodness-of-fit p-values are presented. Plotting retains at most eight paths and downsamples only their display coordinates; moments and histograms use every simulated attacker.
