01 / THE IDEA

One number in.
One curve point out.

Elliptic-curve scalar multiplication connects a secret number to a public point. Before using the simulator, it helps to understand the curve, the arithmetic and what makes this a one-way operation.

In public-key cryptography, a private key is a secret number k . A public key is computed from that number using a publicly known point G . The operation is written Q = kG . Anyone who knows k and G can calculate Q, but recovering k from Q is computationally difficult on a properly chosen, large curve.

For this exercise we use y² ≡ x³ + 7 (mod 17) . Here “mod 17” means that we only care about the remainder after dividing by 17: each coordinate lies between 0 and 16. The equation has 17 ordinary points, plus a special point O called the point at infinity. We use G = (1, 5) and begin with k = 6 .

Important: multiplying a point by 6 is not multiplying its x and y coordinates by 6. It means adding the point to itself six times, using the elliptic-curve addition rule.

04 / STATISTICAL EXPLORATION

What do the numbers reveal?

The first experiment produces a point. This one produces a dataset: calculate Q for k = 1, 2, …, N, then look at coordinate frequencies, bit patterns and input–output relationships.

Statistics asks whether outputs appear evenly spread and how they change when the input changes. This does not prove that a cryptographic algorithm is secure. Our curve is extremely small and its output sequence repeats, so it is a convenient example of why mathematical structure matters when interpreting numbers.

Choose how many private keys to test, then select a chart to read its explanation. Frequencies and bit percentages use only finite points : the special point O has no coordinates, so it is counted separately.

05 / INTERPRETATION

What have we learned?

Our results tell a more interesting story than simply “the algorithm works”. They show what elliptic-curve multiplication does, what statistics can reveal, and what cannot be inferred from a toy example.

We started with a secret integer k and a public base point G, then used elliptic-curve point addition to compute the public point Q = kG. The example k = 6 gives Q = (5, 8). This transformation is deterministic : the same private key and base point always produce the same public key. On real curves with carefully selected parameters, calculating Q is efficient, while recovering k from Q is believed to be computationally infeasible. That asymmetry is why elliptic curves are useful for public-key cryptography and digital signatures.

The statistical experiment reveals another important aspect: the output points have mathematical structure . They must lie on the curve, and multiplying a fixed G visits only its subgroup. The default point has order 9, so outputs repeat after nine steps. As a result, coordinate frequencies are not necessarily uniform, the bits may be unbalanced and large samples may contain many repetitions. These are real properties of our small example, but they cannot be extrapolated directly to Ethereum's much larger secp256k1 curve.

We also compared inputs and outputs using Pearson correlation and changes in their binary representations. These measurements may show patterns, yet weak linear correlation is not proof of randomness , and a large number of changed bits is not proof of security. In particular, elliptic-curve scalar multiplication is not a cryptographic hash function . Hash functions are designed for different tasks, such as producing fixed-size digests with preimage and collision resistance; avalanche-style diffusion is a useful hash-analysis property. Elliptic-curve multiplication instead preserves the algebraic group relationship between keys.

The main lesson is to match each measurement to the property being studied. The forward calculation demonstrates how a public key is obtained. Frequencies, entropy and correlations describe our experimental output. Neither an attractive-looking histogram nor a low correlation coefficient proves that a system is one-way or cryptographically secure. This distinction prepares us for Homework 2, where public-key cryptography, key derivation, hashing and checksums have different roles in the Ethereum wallet pipeline.

SCOPE OF THE HOMEWORK

The toy curve is a teaching tool. All numerical results depend on the chosen base point and sample; they are not security guarantees.
