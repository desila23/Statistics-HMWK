Exercise 1

Riemann and Lebesgue integration

Compare the same function using two partition sizes, absolute error, observed convergence and measured cost.

Partition the domain

Divide [a,b] into n equal intervals of width Δx. Evaluate at their midpoints. For a bounded Riemann-integrable function, tagged sums converge to the same integral as the mesh shrinks.

Iₙ = Δx ∑ f(a + (i + ½)Δx),   i = 0,…,n−1.

Partition the range

For nonnegative f, let S(y) = m({x : f(x) > y}), the length of the measurable superlevel set. The difference S(u) − S(v) measures the preimage of the range bin (u,v]. Integrating S over heights counts the same area by horizontal slices.

∫Df dx=∫0∞S(y)dy

Apply midpoint quadrature to S on [0,T]. Because S decreases, right and left endpoints bound the clipped integral from below and above, up to floating-point error.

What changes between the methods?

Smooth functions often favor midpoint sums in the domain. Range quadrature can converge more slowly when S has an endpoint cusp, as for x². An aligned grid can integrate a step exactly; a misaligned grid can change the result in either direction. More subdivisions need not improve every individual estimate.

For a bounded Riemann-integrable function, the two integrals agree. For 1/√x the improper domain integral agrees with Lebesgue integration, but refining height bins alone cannot remove a fixed clipping error. For Dirichlet, rational-only sampling is misleading; range bins use exact measures rather than counting rational grid points. Thomae’s function is Riemann-integrable despite its dense discontinuities.

Computationally, domain sums use n function evaluations. This range implementation uses 2k+1 superlevel queries to compute both bounds and a midpoint estimate. Each query is cheap when a closed formula is available. For sinc it can require 48 function evaluations. A generic range algorithm would need to estimate preimage measures, potentially adding another layer of numerical error.

Exercise 2

Compute the standard normal CDF

Compare four numerical methods at a point or across a reference grid. Report both Φ(z) and the smaller one-sided tail q = Φ(−|z|), so rounding a probability to 1 does not hide a small tail.

Exact representation

Φ(z)=12[1+erf(z2)]

Substitute t = u/√2 in the normal density integral. The error function erf(x) is 2/√π times the integral of e⁻ᵗ² from 0 to x. This is an exact identity. A practical evaluator still needs a numerical approximation of erf or erfc.

Efficient method: series and continued fraction

Let x = z²/2. Then q = Q(½,x)/2, where Q is the regularized upper incomplete gamma ratio. For x < 1.5, use a positive-term lower-gamma series, then take its complement. Otherwise evaluate the upper-gamma continued fraction directly with modified Lentz iteration.

The direct tail avoids subtracting nearly equal numbers at large |z|. Terms or fraction updates stop near binary64 precision. Near |z| ≈ √3 the fraction needs more iterations; deeper in the tail it converges faster.

Quadrature baseline

Integrate the density φ(t) = e⁻ᵗ²⁄²/√(2π) over [0,|z|]. Composite trapezoid and Simpson use fixed steps. For fixed z and smooth φ, their usual error orders are O(h²) and O(h⁴), respectively; particular endpoints can cancel leading terms. Both compute q by subtracting the central area from ½, which loses relative accuracy for small tails.

Direct erf power series

erf(x) = (2/√π) ∑ₖ₌₀∞ (−1)ᵏ x²ᵏ⁺¹ / [k!(2k+1)].

Integrate the exponential power series term by term. The series converges for every real x, but large alternating terms cancel in finite precision. This demo restricts |z| to 6 for the direct series; the limit is numerical, not mathematical.

Why a special function?

Liouville’s theorem and the Gaussian

Liouville’s theorem on elementary antiderivatives says that if an elementary primitive exists, its derivative can be written as a derivative in an algebraic extension plus a finite sum of constant multiples of logarithmic derivatives. Taking trace and norm descends this representation to the base field.

Here take the base field ℂ(x,t), with t = e⁻ˣ²⁄² and t′ = −xt. Expand the rational terms in t at t = ∞. Logarithmic derivatives have no positive powers of t there: their leading factor is a(x)tᵈ, whose logarithmic derivative is a′/a − dx. The coefficient of t in the remaining derivative must therefore equal 1.

If the t coefficient of the primitive’s rational part is R(x), then R′(x) − xR(x) = 1.

No rational R solves this equation. At a finite pole, R′ has a pole of one order higher than xR, so it cannot be canceled. With no finite poles, R is a polynomial. For a nonzero polynomial, −xR has degree one higher than R and cannot cancel R′ to produce a constant. R = 0 also fails.

Thus the Gaussian density has no elementary antiderivative, and neither does its CDF as a function of z. The erf identity is exact because erf names a special function defined by an integral. The numerical methods above turn that identity into usable numbers.
