01 / Modes of convergence

Four meanings of “approaches.”

Write Yₙ for a sequence of random variables and Y for its limit. For the first three modes the variables share a probability space so the difference Yₙ − Y is defined. Convergence in distribution only compares laws.

In probability

P(|Yₙ − Y| > ε) → 0 for every ε > 0.

At a large fixed n, deviations are unlikely. A single path may still deviate infinitely often. This is the conclusion of the weak law of large numbers and a standard notion of estimator consistency.

Almost surely

P({ω : limₙ Yₙ(ω) = Y(ω)}) = 1.

On almost every path, for each ε the deviations are eventually below ε forever. The starting index can depend on the path and ε. This is the conclusion of the strong law.

In mean square (L²)

E[(Yₙ − Y)²] → 0.

Squared errors shrink on average for square-integrable variables. Large errors carry more weight. For an estimator of a constant, mean squared error equals variance plus squared bias.

In distribution

Fₙ(t) → F(t) at every continuity point of F.

The laws approach a limiting law, without a claim of closeness on any individual path. The classical CLT gives this convergence for a standardized sum. If the limit is a constant, distributional convergence also implies convergence in probability.

Which implications hold?

Almost surely ⇒ in probability ⇒ in distribution
L² ⇒ in probability

Almost sure convergence implies convergence in probability by bounded convergence applied to deviation indicators. For L², Markov’s inequality bounds P(|Yₙ−Y| > ε) by E[(Yₙ−Y)²]/ε². For convergence in probability, CDF values are bounded using F(t−ε), F(t+ε) and the deviation probability; continuity at t gives the distributional limit.

02 / LLN, CLT and the square root

Same average. Different question.

The laws of large numbers tell us where a sample average goes. The central limit theorem describes the shape of the remaining error after rescaling.

Why √n?

For Sₙ = ΣXᵢ, Var(Sₙ) = ΣVar(Xᵢ) + 2Σᵢ<ⱼ Cov(Xᵢ,Xⱼ). Independence makes the covariances zero when second moments exist; pairwise uncorrelatedness is enough for that calculation.

Var(Sₙ) = nσ²;   SD(Sₙ) = σ√n.
Var(X̄ₙ) = σ²/n;   SE(X̄ₙ) = σ/√n.

For independent observations of common variance σ², divide the centered sum by its SD, or multiply the centered average by √n/σ. Both produce variance one. Variance additivity alone does not imply a CLT.

What each theorem requires

For i.i.d. observations with E|X₁| < ∞, X̄ₙ converges almost surely to μ by the strong LLN, hence also in probability by the weak LLN. A finite variance gives the extra identity E[(X̄ₙ−μ)²] = σ²/n: this proves L² convergence and gives the elementary Chebyshev proof of the weak law.

Zₙ = (Sₙ − nμ)/(σ√n) = √n(X̄ₙ − μ)/σ ⇒ N(0,1).

The classical i.i.d. CLT assumes 0 < σ² < ∞. It asserts convergence in distribution for Zₙ, not that the scaled error vanishes or approaches a normal variable path by path.

When independence changes the answer

Suppose n variables of variance σ² have the same nonnegative pairwise correlation ρ. Then Var(X̄ₙ) = σ²[ρ + (1−ρ)/n]. At ρ = 1 all observations can be copies of one random variable; the average then has the original variance and does not concentrate around μ. Some dependent processes still admit √n scaling with a different variance, under additional assumptions.

In inference, the standard error controls the precision of a sample mean and is estimated by s/√n under i.i.d. sampling. The CLT supports approximate confidence intervals and tests when its conditions are appropriate. LLN-type consistency of s and Slutsky’s theorem justify replacing σ by s asymptotically.

Bernoulli sampling lab

Every path uses successive prefixes of one i.i.d. Bernoulli sequence. The exact distribution of Sₙ is Binomial(n,p), so the same model yields exact deviation probabilities, exact MSE and the standardized CDF.

Probability outside the ε band

Exact and empirical tails use |X̄ₙ−p| > ε. Small n can show lattice effects. Zero simulated deviations does not imply a zero probability.

CLT: compare CDFs

The discrete CDF is exact up to floating-point rounding. The normal curve uses a numerical approximation (absolute error about 8 × 10⁻⁸ or less). The metric reports their largest absolute gap, checking both sides of each jump.

03 / Conditioning and Bayes’ theorem

Update a probability after evidence.

In probability, conditioning restricts the reference population to outcomes consistent with the evidence. In statistics, that conditional model describes data under a hypothesis, or parameters under observed data.

Conditional probability

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B)/P(B), for P(B) > 0.

Reversing the conditioning generally changes the question. P(alert | incident) describes detector sensitivity; P(incident | alert) asks how credible an alert is.

Bayes’ rule

P(H | D) = P(D | H)P(H)/P(D).

The prior P(H) describes uncertainty before D. The likelihood P(D | H) scores how compatible the observed D is with H. The posterior P(H | D) is updated uncertainty. The evidence P(D) normalizes the result using all competing hypotheses.

04 / Bayesian inference and sensitivity

Same observations, different priors.

Estimate an unknown event rate θ from k events among n trials. Given θ, the trials are assumed independent with a common event probability.

Prior × likelihood

For fixed data, the binomial likelihood is L(θ) ∝ θᵏ(1−θ)ⁿ⁻ᵏ. It is a function of θ, not a probability distribution over θ. Using a Beta(a,b) prior multiplies that kernel by θᵃ⁻¹(1−θ)ᵇ⁻¹.

π(θ | D) = L(θ)π(θ) / ∫₀¹ L(u)π(u)du
θ | D ∼ Beta(a+k, b+n−k).

What the posterior gives you

The posterior summarizes uncertainty, supports credible intervals, and gives a probability for the next success by averaging θ over the posterior. Under this model, the posterior mean is the predictive probability and, for n>0, equals a weighted average of a/(a+b) and k/n, with weights a+b and n.

A 95% equal-tailed credible interval contains 95% posterior mass under the chosen prior and likelihood. It is not a statement about the long-run coverage of a frequentist confidence procedure.

Applications, benefits and limits

Use past studies or engineering knowledge to form a prior, and a sampling model to form the likelihood. Sequential updates can carry one posterior forward as the next prior if the model correctly accounts for dependence between batches and does not reuse evidence. This is useful for estimating event rates and interpreting rare alerts.

A prior can stabilize estimates from small samples, while a concentrated or poorly justified prior can pull them away from what the data alone suggest. A misspecified likelihood (for example, treating clustered observations as independent) can make uncertainty look too small. Compare plausible priors and check the fit of the data model. Larger informative samples often reduce sensitivity to well-supported priors; they do not repair a wrong model.
